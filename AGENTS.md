# Repository guidance

## Collaboration and user-provided facts

- Treat facts, measurements, equipment capabilities, and constraints explicitly supplied by the user as established project inputs. Carry them into instructions and designs without downgrading them to assumptions, adding blanket verification caveats, or repeatedly asking for confirmation.
- Clearly distinguish user-provided facts from agent-selected defaults, estimates, and general engineering heuristics. Qualify only the uncertain claim; do not let a caveat about a heuristic cast doubt on established inputs.
- Speak up when concrete evidence, an inconsistency, or a specific engineering failure mode indicates a problem. State the conflicting evidence or mechanism, its practical consequence, and a proposed resolution. Generic uncertainty alone is not a reason to challenge a supplied fact or hold up progress.
- Apply verification to the work produced: for example, check that a designed part fits the user's stated build volume. Do not turn that check into a demand to re-establish the build volume itself. Preserve established inputs unless the user revises them or concrete conflicting evidence requires discussion.

## Human-readable, idiomatic design and implementation

- Build for humans to understand and follow the **principle of least surprise** in every artifact. Use the idioms, structure, naming, and workflows customary to the tool and discipline so an experienced practitioner encountering the project for the first time can readily understand and maintain it.
- Prefer straightforward, conventional solutions with clear intent. Avoid clever shortcuts, opaque generated structures, unnecessary abstractions, and tool-specific workarounds that make the result harder to inspect or edit. When a departure from convention is necessary, document the concrete reason and its implications near the affected implementation or in the relevant design documentation.
- For firmware, use recognizable embedded-development conventions: cohesive modules, descriptive symbols, explicit hardware mappings and units, understandable control flow and timing, and clear ownership of state and hardware resources. Make interrupt behavior and concurrency visible where applicable. A firmware engineer should be able to trace operation without reconstructing hidden assumptions.
- For FreeCAD, produce an editable design with an intuitive model tree: meaningful document, body, sketch, feature, and parameter names; logical grouping; and clear dependencies and design intent. Use native modeling workflows appropriate to the geometry, following established FreeCAD practices. Keep construction/reference geometry and assembly structure understandable; do not leave unexplained intermediate objects or substitute opaque geometry for a maintainable feature history without a concrete reason.
- For KiCad, follow established schematic and PCB organization conventions: readable functional grouping and signal flow, consistent references and net names, clear power and connector interfaces, and conventional library/project structure. An experienced KiCad user should be able to navigate the sources, trace a circuit, and locate its physical implementation without relying on agent-only context.
- Treat readability and idiomatic structure as acceptance criteria alongside functional correctness. Before handing off a change, inspect the artifact as its next human maintainer would encounter it, including native CAD trees and schematics rather than only exports or automated checks.

## Project files and temporary work

- Keep final requirements, implementation documentation, and supporting evidence in `docs/`; keep intentional manufacturing deliverables in `manufacturing/`.
- Put intermediate working artifacts, temporary exports, routing candidates, logs, disposable fixtures, downloaded inspection copies, and agent-only scripts in the repository-root `.agent-scratch/` directory. Do not scatter them through project directories.
- Use a task-specific subdirectory within `.agent-scratch/`. Keep it clean during work and remove obsolete or reproducible temporary files when the task finishes. Retain only scratch material needed for active work or an explicitly requested local archive, and identify any retained material in the handoff.
- Before deleting or recursively moving scratch content, verify the resolved absolute path is inside this repository's `.agent-scratch/`. Never delete unrelated user files.
- `.agent-scratch/` is ignored by Git. Final documentation and committed tools must not depend on files there. Promote necessary final evidence to an appropriate project directory before referencing it.
- Put intentionally maintained, reusable project scripts in `tools/`, with usage and dependencies documented. Agent-only experiments belong in `.agent-scratch/`, even when written as scripts.
- Ignore Python bytecode and cache directories. Do not commit automatic backups, local preferences, lock files, or editor history.
- Preserve unrelated user changes. Do not stage, commit, or remove files unless requested.

## KiCad design integrity

- Follow the applicable KiCad/Konnect skills. Use Konnect tools for modifications to KiCad design files, libraries, and library tables; do not edit their serialized contents with text manipulation.
- Keep design sources, required project libraries, library tables, and design rules under version control.
- Distinguish final verification evidence from historical results; do not present an earlier revision's checks as validation of the current design.
- Take care not to specify components that cannot be easily sourced. No design meets its minimum bar if it requires components that cannot be obtained
- Strive for the simplest solution that works - your best work is what can be easily described to a learner and understood

## Design for 3D printing

- **Maintain a bias toward printing what we can and only fall back to purchased materials with clear justification.** Evaluate integrated printed features first, then separate printed parts, before adding purchased mechanical items. Carryover from an earlier BOM or convenience alone is not sufficient justification.
- For each purchased mechanical item, document the specific functional advantage over a printed solution, such as grip/compliance, wear resistance, precision, temperature performance, or repeated fastening. Apply the printing bias within the project's strength, electrical insulation, clearance, printability, assembly, and service requirements. Review standoffs, washers, feet, cable restraints, and fasteners explicitly rather than assuming they must be purchased.
- Include required printed replacements and representative fit coupons in the primary multi-plate 3MF workflow, sharing filament/process settings with the enclosure. Keep item-specific print-versus-buy decisions and unresolved prototype checks in the decision register.
- Let the job determine the number of plates. Use as few plates as practical without compromising fit testing, print orientation, material/color grouping or assembly workflow; never turn a previous revision's plate count into a fixed requirement.
- Keep the fit-coupon plate a quick, independent sizing aid within the same 3MF project. Do not require printing assembly-critical production parts just to run the fit tests. Use representative coupons and optional duplicate test parts where useful.
- Prefer one color/filament type per plate when practical, including separate plates for contrasting printed accents. The user's printer has an AMS; use it when beneficial, while accounting for filament-change time and purge waste. Shared process settings do not require every object or plate to use the same filament slot; keep coupons representative of the production material/settings they validate.
- Default to FDM/FFF manufacturing. Constrain every individual printed part to a **256 × 256 × 256 mm** build volume in its intended print orientation. Check the oriented bounding box, not just assembly dimensions. Reserve space for any brim, supports, and printer-specific excluded areas; a nominal 256 mm part is not automatically printable. Split oversized designs into mechanically suitable, alignable parts.
- Decide print orientation early. For parts with meaningful loads, identify load directions and likely failure modes, and orient continuous extrusion paths to carry tension/bending while minimizing loads that peel layers apart. Consider screw preload, connector insertion, cable pulls, clips, and drops. Document orientation/material tradeoffs; do not assume infill compensates for weak layer adhesion.
- Deliver individual print meshes already in the recommended orientation, with the bed face on Z = 0 and no geometry below the bed. Preserve assembly coordinates in the editable CAD model and provide separate print-oriented exports. Record which face contacts the bed and why; users should be able to import and print without guessing a rotation.
- Avoid abrupt section changes and sharp internal corners at stressed roots; use fillets, ribs, or gussets where useful. Right angles are acceptable where functional and printable: do not round every edge indiscriminately. Prefer chamfers on downward-facing edges when a fillet would create a difficult overhang.
- Prefer geometry that prints without supports. Use roughly **45° from vertical** as a conservative initial overhang limit, subject to the actual printer/material/profile. Keep bridges short and validate their span; provide support for isolated islands. Redesign horizontal holes with suitable teardrop/chamfered roofs or change orientation when needed. Any required supports must be accessible for removal and avoid critical fit, sealing, and visible surfaces where practical.
- Give each part a stable bed contact area. Consider warping, tall/slender instability, first-layer expansion (elephant foot), and support/brim removal. Relieve mating edges at the bed when needed. Do not assume a broad, thin enclosure panel will remain flat.
- Select material for service temperature, creep under sustained load, impact, UV exposure, and the printing environment. Establish nozzle size, line width, layer height, and wall/perimeter assumptions before finalizing small features. Size walls, bosses, ribs, and skins to produce continuous toolpaths; review sliced output rather than relying on a universal minimum thickness or infill percentage.
- Parameterize fit clearances and distinguish per-side/radial clearance from total/diametral clearance. Allow for printer/material variation, hole undersizing, shrinkage, and different accuracy in XY and Z. Do not promise a press fit, snap fit, or thread fit from nominal CAD dimensions alone; use small representative fit coupons before printing a full enclosure.
- Design fasteners and joints for assembly and service: allow tool access, adequate surrounding material and edge distance, and resistance to boss splitting and pull-through. Use inserts, captive nuts, or through-bolts where repeated assembly warrants them. Use the actual hardware manufacturer's dimensions and insertion requirements; keep heat-set insert installation clear of electronics. Avoid making thin printed threads or brittle clips the default load-bearing connection.
- Check the complete installed envelope: PCB undersides and solder joints, tallest components, mounting hardware, connector bodies, mating plugs, cable bend radii, USB access, controls/displays, and assembly/removal paths. Use verified dimensions and explicit allowances. Never move PCB holes or change electrical clearances merely to simplify an enclosure without reviewing that design change.
- Keep editable, parameterized CAD sources under version control, with units in millimeters and meaningful feature/part names. Export closed, manifold meshes at sufficient resolution for functional curves, and check units, part count, dimensions, and orientation after export. Place final print deliverables and their print notes under `manufacturing/`; keep design rationale and verification under `docs/`.
- Before calling a revision print-ready, inspect a slice using the intended printer/material profile for missing thin features, unsupported regions, wall/skin continuity, supports, bed fit, and estimated time/material. Identify physical checks still pending. Verify critical fits and mechanically significant features on a prototype; distinguish CAD checks, slicer checks, and physical acceptance, always tied to the current revision.

General reference: [Prusa — Modeling with 3D printing in mind](https://help.prusa3d.com/article/modeling-with-3d-printing-in-mind_164135). The **256 × 256 × 256 mm build volume is an established user-provided constraint**. The suggested 45° overhang threshold is an agent-selected starting heuristic and may be refined for the chosen printing profile.
